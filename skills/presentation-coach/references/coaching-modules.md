# Coaching modules and the demo booth playbook

Eleven short modules drawn from the free public videos and articles of Vanessa Van Edwards (V1 to V4) and Vinh
Giang (G1 to G7), plus a demo booth playbook mode. Every module is this skill's own paraphrase and drill, written
from the source passage listed under it; it is not either expert's method, course or endorsement. Paid material
(courses, member programs, paid events) was not used. Source dates, transcript status and research strength are in
`coaching-evidence.md`, section "Module sources".

Each module gives: **when to use it**, **drill**, **success** (what you can observe on a re-take), **source** (link
with timestamp) and **type**: *practitioner* (the creator's experience and advice), *research* (a published study),
or *synthesis* (this skill's combination, credited to nobody).

Rules that apply to all of them:

- Pick at most two modules per rehearsal. They are tools for an observed need, not a curriculum to run in order.
- A drill's success line is checked on a recording or by a listener. Without that evidence, report it **not run**.
- Never ask the speaker to adopt either expert's persona, catchphrases or stories. Adapt to the speaker's culture,
  accent, disability, neurotype and comfort; a drill they decline is skipped, not marked failed.
- The stories, examples and numbers stay the speaker's own. A module can ask for a story; it never writes one.

## Vanessa Van Edwards lens

### V1. Booth and event conversation openers

**When:** booth staff, networking, the first 30 seconds of a demo, or anyone who freezes when a stranger walks up.

**What the sources teach:** keep the first line simple (a plain hello and your name is enough), then ask an open
question that lets the other person talk about their own work. She suggests replacing "What do you do?" with
"Working on anything exciting recently?", asking about the shared context (the event, the venue, what they are
holding), swapping "fun" or "big" for "interesting", and asking for advice. With introverts, she reports that
"What's your story?" landed badly in her lab, so start with a context question, leave silence after you ask, and
preface an unusual question ("Here's a fun question..."). Have your own answer to every question ready. Her 2019 list
includes "What's your story?" as a starter; her 2022 introvert video advises against it. For strangers at a booth,
follow the later, introvert-safe advice.

**Drill:** write three openers for this event: one context question, one "working on anything interesting" variant
and one advice question. Practise each on a colleague, then stay quiet for a full three seconds after asking.
Answer each question yourself in one sentence.

**Success:** in a five-visitor role-play, the visitor talks first about their own work within the first two
exchanges, and the staffer never opens with "What do you do?" or "What's your story?".

**Source:** [Conversation starters for introverts, 0:14-2:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fmP5ZzBWrAk&t=14s);
[Killer conversation starters, 0:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6K9LhzyLUfY&t=50s) and
[8:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6K9LhzyLUfY&t=534s);
[Diary of a CEO interview, 84:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VHUrdELKjDw&t=5058s) and
[152:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VHUrdELKjDw&t=9161s);
[Introvert conversation starters](https://www.scienceofpeople.com/introvert-conversation-starters/);
[Conversation starters and topics](https://www.scienceofpeople.com/conversation-starters-topics/).
**Type:** practitioner. The lab finding about "What's your story?" is her report; no study was found to read.

### V2. Metaphor check

**When:** a talk introduces a concept the audience has not met (a new product idea, an architecture, a process).

**What the sources teach:** in her public-speaking tips she calls metaphors, similes and analogies mini-stories that
tie something the listener already knows to a new idea, and lists them among charismatic verbal tactics. The
research she points to (Antonakis and colleagues, 2011) trained managers in a set of charismatic leadership tactics
that included metaphor, and raters judged the trained group more charismatic.

**Drill:** list every new concept in the talk. For each, write one familiar comparison in a single sentence, then
test it on someone outside the field: ask them to explain the concept back using the comparison. Keep one metaphor
per concept; cut mixed or stacked metaphors. Check the comparison does not imply something false (a speed, a
guarantee, a scale).

**Success:** a listener outside the field restates each new concept correctly using its metaphor, and no concept
carries two competing metaphors.

**Source:** [15 science-based public speaking tips, tip #1](https://medium.com/@vvanedwards/15-science-based-public-speaking-tips-to-become-a-master-speaker-1cf01bedd6c8)
(article, no timestamp). Research: [Antonakis, Fenley and Liechti (2011), *Can charisma be taught?*](https://doi.org/10.5465/amle.2010.0012).
**Type:** practitioner advice backed by one field study of a tactic bundle; the study does not isolate metaphor,
so do not claim metaphor alone raises ratings, and do not repeat her percentage figure as a fact.

### V3. Imagery cues: visuals, colour and props

**When:** a deck or booth whose look may contradict the script, for example a warm spoken opening over a dense,
cold wall of numbers, or a product demo where the props have never been rehearsed.

**What the sources teach:** she describes four channels of cues: body language, vocal, verbal (word choice), and a
smaller "ornaments" channel of colours, clothing and accessories. In her tips she extends nonverbal signals to what
you wear and carry (for example, arriving with one bag rather than several), and her rehearsal advice has a
separate phase for movement, body language and props, naming product demos as the case people skip.

**Drill:** put the opening slide, the evidence slide and the closing slide side by side. For each, write the signal
the script intends (warmth: "we are on your side"; competence: "this is sourced and works") and the signal the
visual sends. Fix the mismatches on the slide, not in the script: a human or audience-centred image where the words
are about the audience, a clean sourced chart where the words claim evidence, one accent colour used consistently
for the one message. Then rehearse once with every prop and click, in the real outfit.

**Success:** a colleague shown the three slides without audio names the intended signal for each; the prop and
click rehearsal runs with no fumbled hand-offs.

**Source:** [Diary of a CEO interview, 6:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VHUrdELKjDw&t=404s);
[15 science-based public speaking tips, tips #5 and #13](https://medium.com/@vvanedwards/15-science-based-public-speaking-tips-to-become-a-master-speaker-1cf01bedd6c8).
Pointer only, transcript not obtained: [10 presentation ideas, chapters "Change modalities" 2:29 and "Get creative
with color" 5:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Y289da0Gds&t=335s).
**Type:** practitioner. No research reviewed here establishes what a given colour signals; treat colour as
consistency and contrast, never as a universal meaning. Visual redesign belongs to the deck owner; flag it.

### V4. Conversation flow for short demos: open, qualify, show, close

**When:** a booth demo, a hallway demo or the first five minutes of a customer call.

**What the sources teach:** her ten-step conversation article sets an intention before the event (who, what, when,
why), approaches with open body language and visible hands, uses a simple or context opener, looks for "sparks"
(topics that raise energy, often signalled by raised eyebrows), backs claims with a short story, keeps talking and
listening reciprocal, "bookmarks" a follow-up, exits gracefully, and reviews afterwards (what went well, what you
learned, whom to follow up).

**This skill's flow** (synthesis): **Open** with V1. **Qualify** with one question that chooses the path ("What
would you want this to do for your team?"). **Show** only the module that answers what they said, with one short
story or proof point. **Close** with a bookmark (a specific next step) and a clean exit, then a 30-second
post-mortem note.

**Drill:** run a five-minute demo three times with a colleague playing a different visitor each time. Record which
module you chose after the qualifying question and why.

**Success:** each run shows a different module chosen from the visitor's answer, the visitor talks for at least a
third of the time, and every run ends with a named next step.

**Source:** [10 steps to the perfect conversation](https://medium.com/@vvanedwards/10-steps-to-the-perfect-conversation-44f65616e950)
(article, no timestamp).
**Type:** practitioner steps; the open-qualify-show-close mapping is this skill's synthesis.

## Vinh Giang lens

### G1. Vocal image audit: the first-recording exercise

**When:** the first rehearsal with a recording, or a speaker who says "I hate hearing myself".

**What the sources teach:** people judge your visual image first and your vocal image as soon as you speak. He
treats the voice as an instrument and calls unfamiliar sounds unfamiliar, not fake. He explains that you hear
yourself partly through bone, so a recording sounds strange, and the fix is repeated listening until the two match.
His review routine: record five minutes of improvised speaking, wait a day, then review three times: sound only
(the vocal image), muted video (the visual image), and a transcript with filler words left in and highlighted.
When he reviews students he checks each vocal foundation (rate, volume, pitch and melody, tonality, pause), for
example volume that stays in a narrow safe band.

**Drill:** follow that three-pass review on a five-minute improvised take. Write one thing you like and one
limitation per pass. Pick one foundation to work on, and note discomfort with your own voice as expected, not as a
finding.

**Success:** three written notes per pass exist, one foundation is chosen, and the next take's same pass shows a
change you can point to (a timestamp). The speaker's dislike of their voice is acknowledged, not graded.

**Source:** [13 years of communication skills in 53 minutes, 0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=0s),
[3:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=181s), [4:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=246s),
[13:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=793s) and [51:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=3082s).
**Type:** practitioner. The bone-conduction explanation is his summary of a known effect; this skill did not review
a study on it.

### G2. Pre-session nerves routine

**When:** the hour before a talk, a booth shift or a recorded take.

**What the sources teach:** nerves often come from not knowing the content, so rehearse the way you will present
(his "table read": read it aloud as if the audience is there, not mumbled to a mirror). Remember the audience is
mostly thinking about their own needs, not judging you. Shift attention to serving the audience. Before going on he
calms breathing, burns off adrenaline with brisk movement so it does not turn into pacing, and warms up the mouth
with lip trills through a song and a low-to-high "siren".

**Drill:** build a five-minute routine from those parts the speaker is comfortable with: two minutes of calm
breathing they already know is safe for them, one minute of brisk walking, two minutes of lip trills or sirens, then
one sentence answering "What does this audience need from me today?". Run it before every recorded rehearsal so it
is familiar on the day.

**Success:** the speaker can run the routine unprompted, the first 60 seconds of the take have no aimless pacing,
and the speaker names the audience need aloud before starting.

**Source:** [13 years in 53 minutes, 23:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=1426s);
[Diary of a CEO interview, 63:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oIiv_335yus&t=3802s) and
[65:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oIiv_335yus&t=3910s).
**Type:** practitioner. Do not prescribe breath-holding or any specific breathing protocol; anyone with a health
concern skips that step. This is not treatment for anxiety.

### G3. Pace fixes for slow and fast speakers

**When:** a timing run over or under budget, or a listener who says "rushed" or "dragged".

**What the sources teach:** vary rate with purpose: slow down for what matters (a "verbal highlight"), speed up
for what matters less. A fixed rate, slow or fast or even engaging, turns monotonous. For slow speakers he separates
"software" (thinking in one language and translating into another adds load) from "hardware" (the mouth is not
used to faster movements; practise tongue twisters slowly, then faster). Fast spurts are fine; fast as a default
becomes hard to follow. He suggests short solo sessions stepping through rates one to ten.

**Drill:** mark the one sentence per slide that matters most and slow it; let the setup around it move faster. For
range, do the one-to-ten rate ladder for four minutes. Then re-run `build_talk_track.py` and a timed out-loud take.
If the talk is over budget, cut words; never fix timing by planning to talk faster.

**Success:** on the re-take, the marked sentences are audibly slower than their surroundings, the `TIMING:` line
still shows at least 10% spare, and the measured run time matches the estimate within the skill's tolerance.

**Source:** [13 years in 53 minutes, 4:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=246s) and
[17:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=1050s);
[4 stages of mastery, 5:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VudpIQVORSI&t=300s).
**Type:** practitioner. There is no universal ideal rate; compare the speaker with themselves. Treat second-language
load with respect: give more out-loud practice, never comment on accent.

### G4. Optional low-risk humour

**When:** only when the speaker wants it, in a setting where a missed joke costs little.

**What the sources teach:** most wit that looks spontaneous was prepared or used before. Humour needs timing,
intelligence and some risk; he explains it with the benign-violation idea (funny when something is a little wrong
but safe). Read the room by listening, and do not force it. If a line misses, move on at once without apologising
at length, and afterwards review why a line worked or failed. A borrowed line can be personalised.

**Drill:** write one optional light line for a moment in the talk that is about the speaker or a shared situation,
never about the audience, a group, a client or a competitor. Mark it "optional" in the script. Rehearse the
move-on line for when it misses.

**Success:** a colleague hears the line and finds nothing that targets a person or group; on a miss in rehearsal,
the speaker continues within two seconds.

**Source:** [13 years in 53 minutes, 30:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=1847s) and
[40:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=2419s). Research: [McGraw and Warren (2010), *Benign
violations*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20587696/).
**Type:** practitioner advice with one supporting line of research on what makes things funny. His "most wit is
prepared" split is his own estimate, not a measured figure. Never required; skip it for high-stakes, cross-cultural
or sensitive audiences.

### G5. Develop your own style and break out of your mould

**When:** a speaker who wants more range, sounds flat, or says "that isn't me".

**What the sources teach:** pick several communicators you admire, list the specific "ingredients" you can now see
(tonality, facial movement, pace changes, gestures), imitate them in practice, then add your own flavour. People
around you may push you back into the version of you they expect; practising somewhere new (he recommends improv
classes) gives room to try a different part of yourself.

**Drill (stretch, never a persona):** choose three communicators the speaker admires. Name one ingredient from
each. Rehearse one 60-second segment three times, borrowing one ingredient per take, then a fourth take keeping only
what felt like the speaker. Optional: one improv or low-stakes practice group.

**Success:** the speaker names which ingredient they kept and why, and the fourth take still sounds like them to a
colleague who knows them.

**Source:** [13 years in 53 minutes, 43:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=2634s) and
[47:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g0kzHjmvuYQ&t=2843s);
[Diary of a CEO interview, 79:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oIiv_335yus&t=4775s).
**Type:** practitioner. Imitation is a practice step only. Never script the speaker into Vinh's, Vanessa's or anyone
else's persona.

### G6. The four stages of mastery as a rehearsal tracker

**When:** across several rehearsals, to show progress on one skill at a time.

**What the sources teach:** the four stages of learning: unconscious incompetence (you don't know what you don't
know), conscious incompetence (you see the gap), conscious competence (you can do it while thinking about it) and
unconscious competence (it is second nature). Progress comes from repetition and review. Any time you speak is a
chance to practise, even alone. The goal is many "gears" you can switch to when the moment needs it, not being "on"
all the time.

**Drill:** keep a one-line tracker per skill being worked on (for example pause, volume range, opener). After each
rehearsal, mark the stage from evidence: "cannot hear it in my take" (1), "hear it, can't fix it yet" (2), "did it
when I thought about it, timestamp" (3), "did it without a prompt in a real session" (4). Change one skill at a
time.

**Success:** each stage change cites a recording timestamp or a listener note; nothing is marked stage 4 from a
rehearsal alone.

**Source:** [4 stages to develop communication skills mastery, 0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VudpIQVORSI&t=0s),
[5:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VudpIQVORSI&t=300s) and [7:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VudpIQVORSI&t=444s);
[blog post](https://vinhgiang.com/blog/4-stages-to-develop-communication-skills-mastery-vinh-giang).
**Type:** practitioner use of a widely taught learning model; the model itself is not his and is not a validated
measure. The tracker is a self-report aid, not a score.

### G7. Storytelling for problem-statement openings

**When:** an opening that states a problem in the abstract ("Teams struggle with X") and does not land.

**What the sources teach:** from his free storytelling workshop and interview: tell the story around the peak
emotional or action moment and cut the rest; be specific (names, what a person looked like and said); play the
characters with a little dialogue; relive the story rather than report it, using visual, auditory, kinaesthetic and
smell details on top of who, what, where and when; then link it by asking "what does this story symbolise?" and
saying why you told it. The story comes first, the link second, the points third.

**Drill:** ask the speaker for a real moment when they or someone they work with hit the problem (ask; never
invent). Retell it in under 45 seconds: start at the peak moment, add one line of dialogue and one sensory detail,
then one link sentence ("I'm telling you this because...") that leads into the problem statement. Record a
"report" version and a "relive" version and compare.

**Success:** a listener can repeat the problem statement after the story; the story is true, approved by the
speaker, inside 45 seconds, and contains no invented detail, figure or customer.

**Source:** [7 powerful storytelling secrets workshop, 2:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YzbzIzgvRLY&t=145s),
[2:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YzbzIzgvRLY&t=172s), [9:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YzbzIzgvRLY&t=569s),
[12:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YzbzIzgvRLY&t=732s), [17:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YzbzIzgvRLY&t=1048s)
and [18:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YzbzIzgvRLY&t=1132s);
[Diary of a CEO interview, 41:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oIiv_335yus&t=2481s) and
[43:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oIiv_335yus&t=2625s).
**Type:** practitioner. Customer stories need the customer's permission and must pass the sensitivity check.

## Demo booth playbook mode

Use when the "talk" is a booth, a stand or a walk-up demo: staff run short, repeated conversations with visitors
who arrive and leave at will. Do not write a timed keynote for a booth. Deliver a playbook card the staff can learn,
built from the sections below, and keep every claim inside the figures file. Read V1, V4 and G2 first.

1. **Hooks (two or three).** One line each, said or shown as people pass, about the visitor's problem, not the
   product. Plus V1 openers for people who stop.
2. **One qualifying question.** One question whose answer picks the path and the first module, for example "What
   are you trying to get done this quarter?". List the two or three answers you expect and the module each one
   routes to.
3. **Modules.** Self-contained demo segments of 60 to 120 seconds, each with:
   - **Click:** the exact screen action or state to start from.
   - **Cue:** the one sentence to say (V2 metaphor if the concept is new).
   - **Point:** where to direct the visitor's eye or hand on the screen.
   - **Proof:** the one sourced fact or short real story (G7), with its figures-file line.
4. **Paths.** **2 minutes:** hook, qualifying question, one module, close. **5 minutes:** add a second module the
   visitor's answer calls for. **10 minutes:** three modules plus questions, or hand off to a scheduled follow-up.
   Each path is a list of module IDs with a time budget that leaves at least 10% spare.
5. **Persona variants.** For each likely visitor (for example a hands-on engineer, a manager, a buyer), the
   qualifying-question answer that identifies them, the module to lead with and the one sentence that changes.
6. **Q&A.** The five most likely questions, skeptical ones first, with short answers; ⚠ on any answer needing a fact
   the owner must confirm. Include "I don't know, here's how I'll find out".
7. **Close.** A bookmark (V4): one named next step the visitor can take, and a graceful exit line.
8. **Reset.** The checklist that returns the demo to its starting state between visitors: screens, data, tabs,
   browser history, props, and anything the last visitor typed. Nothing a visitor typed stays on screen.
9. **Staff rehearsal.** Run each path once against a stopwatch, run the persona variants with a colleague, and do
   the G2 routine before each shift.

Report booth readiness with the same honesty rules as the ready-to-present checks: hygiene and sourcing checks run
on the card itself, timings are estimates until a stopwatch run, and a path never run is **not run**.

Sample (fictional): for the Acme Upgrade Assistant, hook "Still hand-checking every upgrade?", qualifying question
"Which part of upgrades eats your week?", answers routing to modules M1 (Discover and Analyze), M2 (Plan and Dry run, read-only) or
M3 (Approve, Execute and Validate, with automatic rollback), each with its click, cue, point and proof drawn from a
line in `examples/acme-figures.md`.
